Task 1 Warm Up Thinking

  • What features of a speaker's voice help you understand their point of view? Tone of voice, emphasis on words, or speed?
  • In a group discussion, how do you notice that a speaker is about to change their mind or agree with an opponent?
  • Why is it helpful to listen for professional statistics (like percentages) rather than generalizations during scientific debates?
Sample Ideas:
  • Focusing on lexical emphasis helps identify key points. For instance, when speakers put extra stress on words like "concede" or "however," they signaling logical shifts.
  • A change in pitch, or phrases such as "I see your point," typically signals that a speaker is preparing to make a logical concession.
  • Numerical facts provide an objective baseline, which helps listeners evaluate the strength of an argument rather than relying on subjective vocal confidence.

Conceding Phrases: 'I concede that...', 'You make a valid point regarding...', 'That is a fair observation.'

Refuting Phrases: 'I must refute the claim that...', 'The data does not support this...', 'On the contrary, our research indicates...'"

Dr. Kenji: "Welcome to the panel seminar on digital health workflows. Today we are discussing whether automated software systems should replace manual patient triage in emergency departments. Dr. Sarah, please share your view."

Dr. Sarah: "Thank you. Our latest data from the city hospital shows that automated triage algorithms reduce patient waiting times by 40%. Therefore, my CONTENTION is that digital systems are objectively superior at managing patient flow."

Dr. Takashi: "I understand your data, and I CONCEDE that digital triage speeds up the initial registration process. Your time metrics are clear. However, I must REFUTE the claim that these systems are completely superior. Software programs often miss subtle physical signs, such as a patient's unique skin tone or slight changes in breathing rhythm, which an experienced nurse notices instantly."

Dr. Sarah: "I see your point about physical signs. However, the VALIDITY of our study relies on a large sample size where the rate of critical misdiagnosis was extremely low. The software is programmed to flag complex cases for human review anyway."

Dr. Takashi: "That is a fair safety feature. Perhaps we can reach a COMPROMISE. We should not use technology to replace staff, but rather position the digital system as a supportive tool alongside professional triage nurses."

Task 3 Global Stance Listening (Listening for Gist)

  • What central technological workflow are the panelists trying to define?
  • On what specific operational point do Dr. Sarah and Dr. Takashi agree?
  • What is the final middle ground proposed by the end of the audio discussion?
Sample Answers:
  • The Core Argument: The debate explores whether automated software algorithms should replace manual nurse evaluation in triage zones.
  • Shared Agreement: Both speakers agree that automated systems are effective at speeding up the initial administration registration metrics.
  • The Compromise: Dr. Takashi recommends using technology as a supportive digital tool to assist triage nurses, rather than removing human staff.

Task 4 Deep Listening (Listening for Specific Information)

  • Listen for the numeric metric. What precise percentage of wait time is saved by automated software?
  • According to Dr. Takashi, what two visual and physical signs might a program fail to observe?
  • What software guardrail does Dr. Sarah point to that keeps human professionals involved?
Sample Answers:
  • Specific Number: Triage waiting times are reduced by exactly 40%.
  • Physical Details missed: Automated scripts struggle to spot variations in patient skin tone and slight changes in breathing rhythm.
  • Built-in Guardrail: The software automatically highlights or flags complicated conditions to be routed to human staff for priority review.

Task 9 Case Study Decision Making

Scenario:

A hospital board must decide how to spend the remaining annual technology budget.

Option A: Invest completely in the automated triage software to achieve a guaranteed 40% reduction in entry wait times.
Option B: Use the budget to hire three seasonal triage nurses to maintain human oversight, touch, and direct patient interaction.

Debate with your partner which option secures better long-term patient safety and hospital reputation.

Analysis:

A balanced compromise represents the highest level of strategic administrative communication. While Option A optimizes hospital metrics (processing speed), completely excluding nurses creates acute risk when complex clinical exceptions occur. Option B preserves human diagnostic accuracy but lacks structural speed. Combining reduced automation with human supervision yields optimal long-term outcomes.
