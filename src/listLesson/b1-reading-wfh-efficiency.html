WFH Energy-Saving Policy Raises Questions of Fairness

Jakarta – The Indonesian government has announced a new INITIATIVE to reduce national fuel consumption. Under the plan, civil servants and office employees will be encouraged to work from home one day each week. Officials ESTIMATE the measure could cut fuel use by as much as 20 percent, helping the country manage rising global oil prices.

The policy comes at a time of international uncertainty. Conflicts in the Middle East have DISRUPTED oil supplies, pushing prices higher worldwide. By reducing daily commuting, the government hopes to ease pressure on household spending and national energy demand.

While the plan is seen as creative, experts warn it may not benefit all workers equally. The policy is designed for formal employees—those with stable jobs in government offices and private companies. Yet Indonesia’s workforce is DOMINATED by informal workers, such as street vendors, motorcycle taxi drivers, construction laborers, and freelancers. For them, working from home is not an option.

Data from the Ministry of Manpower shows that of 146.5 million workers, only 67.6 million are formally employed. The rest—nearly half—are informal. Many earn less than two million rupiah per month and lack social protection. Rising fuel prices hit them hardest, especially since 95 percent of households rely on motorcycles for daily transport.

Critics argue that the policy risks widening INEQUALITY. Formal workers, who generally enjoy higher incomes and job security, will save money and fuel. Informal workers, however, will continue to face high costs without support. “This could become a privilege for the capable, not a solution for the majority,” one analyst observed.

To make the policy more INCLUSIVE, experts suggest several measures. Improving affordable public transportation could help informal workers reduce fuel use. Offering tax incentives to companies that adopt remote work might encourage wider participation. Finally, tailoring energy-saving strategies to regional needs could make the policy more effective, since fuel consumption patterns vary across Indonesia.

In conclusion, the work-from-home energy policy is a promising step toward addressing global challenges. But without adjustments, it risks leaving millions of informal workers behind. A balanced approach—combining remote work, stronger public transport, and regional energy strategies—may provide a fairer path forward for all Indonesians.
