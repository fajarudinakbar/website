Task 2 Vocabulary Focus

Gen Z in the Workplace

Gen Z refers to people born between the mid-1990s and 2010. They are now starting to work in many companies. This brings new challenges for managers. Some older workers think Gen Z employees are difficult or "too loud," but research shows they just want different things. They care a lot about their mental health, flexible schedules, and doing work that helps the world. Many companies are noticing that these young workers feel very stressed and often suffer from "burnout" (extreme tiredness).

A big survey by Deloitte (2025) asked 23,000 young people what they want. They said they want good careers, but they also want to feel good. Mental health is their number one priority. They also care about the environment and want their companies to be responsible.

In the UK, a study of 2,000 Gen Z workers found that nearly 50% felt very stressed at work. Many of them (39%) thought about quitting their job in the first year. There is also conflict between generations. Older workers sometimes criticize Gen Z for complaining too much or setting boundaries (like not answering phones after work). However, Gen Z workers say they are just protecting their health. Another study found that 83% of Gen Z workers suffer from burnout, which is higher than any other age group.

Key Findings

First, Gen Z cares more about their feelings and health than money or promotions. Second, many young workers feel stressed and tired very early in their careers. Third, flexibility is important; they want to choose when and where they work (for example, working from home). Fourth, there are problems between generations: older colleagues sometimes think Gen Z is unprofessional, while Gen Z thinks older ways of working are unhealthy. Finally, values matter. They like companies that have good ethics and care about the environment.

Recommendations

If companies want to keep their Gen Z employees, they should help employees with stress and mental health. They should also allow flexible working hours. It is important to stop negative stereotypes and encourage talking between older and younger workers. Managers should give them work that is meaningful and helps society. Finally, companies should focus on the results of the work, not just how many hours people sit in the office.

Task 9 Case 1: The "Lazy" Label

Sarah (22) leaves at 4:30. Boss Mike (50) thinks she is lazy. Is she? What should Mike say?

  • Sarah is likely not lazy if her work is good; she values efficiency.
  • Mike is unhappy because he values "hours in seat" (older value).
  • Mike should focus on her results, not the clock, or ask politely if her tasks are done.

Task 10 Case 2: Working from Home

David wants to WFH. Manager refuses. David wants to quit. Compromise?

  • The manager might think presence equals productivity. He is likely wrong according to research (Gen Z is productive remotely).
  • Compromise: Start with 1-2 days WFH per week on a trial basis to prove productivity.

Task 11 Case 3: Stress and Crying

Jenny is crying from stress but won't tell the boss. Why? Advice?

  • She is afraid of looking weak or unprofessional.
  • The text says companies should support mental health and stop stereotypes.
  • Advice: Talk to the manager about prioritizing tasks (not "complaining," but managing workload). Health comes first.
