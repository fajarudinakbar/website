Young Workers and Social Networks in Indonesia

Many young people in Indonesia, especially graduates from VOCATIONAL high schools (SMK), face big challenges when they look for jobs. They often come from lower‑middle class families. Because of this, they do not always have access to OFFICIAL job information or professional networks. Instead, they depend on social connections with family, friends, or neighbors. In Indonesia, this is often called ordal (orang dalam), meaning “someone inside” who can help.

Most young workers do not find jobs through online job portals or school career centers. Instead, they hear about opportunities from people they know. For example, a cousin may tell them about a factory job, or a neighbor may connect them with a shop owner. These strong social ties are very important. Without them, many young people would not know where to start.

The jobs they get through ordal are usually not SECURE. Many are short‑term, without contracts, and often pay less than the minimum wage. Working hours can be irregular, and there are few chances to move up to better positions. This means that even though young workers can enter the job market, they often stay in VULNERABLE situations.

Vocational schools are supposed to prepare students for work. However, many schools do not have strong partnerships with companies. They cannot give students real work experience or connect them with professional networks. As a result, students must rely on their personal connections instead of formal career support.

Social networks are usually strongest within the same economic group. Young people from lower‑middle class families often know others in similar situations. This means they are more likely to get low‑paying jobs. Meanwhile, young people from wealthy families have connections to better opportunities. In this way, social networks reproduce INEQUALITY: the rich stay rich, and the poor remain in vulnerable positions.

This situation shows how social and economic inequality continues in Indonesia. Strong ties help young workers survive, but they do not help them grow. Without wider networks or fairer systems, many young people will remain stuck in insecure jobs. To change this, schools, companies, and the government need to work together. They must create fair opportunities, build professional connections, and support young workers in developing their skills.
