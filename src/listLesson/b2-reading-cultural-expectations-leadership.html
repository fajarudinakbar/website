Cultural Expectations & Leadership

Topic: Business Culture, Management Styles, and Cross-Cultural Communication

Instructions: Use the text boxes to prepare your ideas. Use the "Show Sample" buttons to compare your answers with suggested responses.

Part 1 Warm-up Discussion

1. Think about a past leader. Did they give detailed instructions or expect you to figure it out? Which style did you prefer?

2. In your culture, how do employees show respect? (Following orders silently vs. offering new ideas)

3. Have you ever worked/studied in a team with different communication styles? What happened?

Sample Reflections:
  • Some prefer autonomy (coaching style), while others prefer clarity (directive style) to avoid mistakes.
  • In High Power Distance cultures, silence often equals respect. In Low Power Distance, participation equals respect.
  • Misunderstandings often occur; silence can be mistaken for agreement, or directness for rudeness.

Cultural Expectations and Leadership

Gabriela worked for a multinational company as a successful project manager in Brazil and was transferred to manage a team in Sweden. She was excited about her new role but soon realised that managing her new team would be a challenge. Despite their friendliness, Gabriela didn't feel respected as a leader. Her new staff would question her proposals openly in meetings, and when she gave them instructions on how to carry out a task, they would often go about it in their own way without checking with her. When she announced her decisions on the project, they would continue giving their opinions as if it was still up for discussion.

After weeks of frustration, Gabriela emailed her Swedish manager about the issues she was facing with her team. Her manager simply asked her if she felt her team was still performing, and what she thought would help her better collaborate with her team members. Gabriela found her manager vague and didn't feel as if he was managing the situation satisfactorily.

What Gabriela was experiencing was a cultural clash in expectations. She was used to a more hierarchical framework where the team leader and manager took control and gave specific instructions on how things were to be done. This more directive management style worked well for her and her team in Brazil but did not transfer well to her new team in Sweden, who were more used to a flatter hierarchy where decision making was more democratic. When Gabriela took the issue to her Swedish manager, rather than stepping in with directions about what to do, her manager took on the role of coach and focused on getting her to come up with her own solutions instead.

Dutch social psychologist Geert Hofstede uses the concept of 'power distance' to describe how power is distributed and how hierarchy is perceived in different cultures. In her previous work environment, Gabriela was used to a high power distance culture where power and authority are respected and everyone has their rightful place. In such a culture, leaders make the big decisions and are not often challenged. Her Swedish team, however, were used to working in a low power distance culture where subordinates often work together with their bosses to find solutions and make decisions. Here, leaders act as coaches or mentors who encourage independent thought and expect to be challenged.

When Gabriela became aware of the cultural differences between her and her team, she took the initiative to have an open conversation with them about their feelings about her leadership. Pleased to be asked for their thoughts, Gabriela's team openly expressed that they were not used to being told what to do. They enjoyed having more room for initiative and creative freedom. When she told her team exactly what she needed them to do, they felt that she didn't trust them to do their job well. They realised that Gabriela was taking it personally when they tried to challenge or make changes to her decisions, and were able to explain that it was how they'd always worked.

Part 4 Comprehension Analysis

1. The Conflict: Why did Gabriela feel disrespected? What specific behaviors did they show?

2. The Manager's Role: Why did she describe her manager as "vague"? What was he trying to do?

3. The Resolution: The team felt Gabriela "didn't trust them." Why?

  • Conflict: She felt disrespected because they questioned her proposals openly and did tasks their own way without checking with her.
  • Manager: He seemed vague because he was acting as a coach (Low Power Distance), trying to help her find her own solution rather than giving orders.
  • Resolution: In their culture, being told exactly what to do implies incompetence. They are used to autonomy; specific instructions felt like micromanagement.

Part 5 Speaking Task: Explaining 'Power Distance'

Structure / Diagram Description:

Prompt: "So, Geert Hofstede describes 'power distance' as..."

Sample Explanation:
"So, Geert Hofstede describes 'power distance' as the way power is distributed in a culture. In High Power Distance cultures, like Brazil in the story, hierarchy is strict, and leaders make decisions without being challenged (Director role). In Low Power Distance cultures, like Sweden, the hierarchy is flat. Decisions are democratic, and leaders act more like coaches who expect input from their team."

Part 6 Deep Dive Discussion

"In High Power Distance, leaders make big decisions... In Low Power Distance, leaders act as coaches and expect to be challenged."

1. In your industry, is it dangerous to challenge a leader? Or is it rewarded?

2. Can a "Low Power Distance" style work in a crisis (e.g., military or ER)? Why/Why not?

  • Crisis Situations: Often require directive leadership (High Power Distance) because speed is critical. Debate wastes time in an emergency.
  • Creative Industries: Often require Low Power Distance to foster innovation.

Part 7 Individual Task: Strategic Communication

Context: You are Gabriela. You called a meeting to fix the team dynamic. Prepare a 2-minute monologue.

Planning: List 3 key points (diplomatic language)

"Hi everyone, thank you for joining me. I wanted to have an open conversation about how we work together. I've noticed some friction recently, and I realise now that it might come from our different cultural backgrounds. Coming from Brazil, I'm used to a style where I give clear instructions to support my team. However, I understand that here, you value autonomy and collaboration. My intention was never to micromanage or show a lack of trust. I want us to find a middle ground. How would you prefer we handle task assignments moving forward?"

Part 8 Problem Solving (Reverse Culture Shock)

Scenario: A Swedish manager in a High Power Distance culture asks the team: "I don't know. What do you think?" The team is silent. Why?

Analysis: The team is uncomfortable because in High Power Distance cultures, the manager is expected to have the answers. Asking the team might look like incompetence or weakness.

Solution: The manager should state the problem clearly, offer 1-2 tentative options, and then ask for specific feedback on those options, rather than asking for an open-ended solution immediately.

Part 9 "The Trust Paradox" Discussion

How can a manager give instructions/check work without making people feel untrusted? Suggest 3 polite phrases/strategies.

  • "I'm here if you need support, otherwise I'll let you run with it."
  • "Let's agree on the final outcome, and I'll leave the 'how' up to you."
  • "Could you update me on Friday just so I can keep the client informed?" (Framing the check-up as external necessity).
