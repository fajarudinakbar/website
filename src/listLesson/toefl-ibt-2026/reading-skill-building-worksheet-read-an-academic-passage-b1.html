At the B1 (Intermediate) level, texts will cover clear, concrete academic concepts. You need to identify main ideas, locate specific facts, and guess the meaning of words from context. Reading an academic textbook is different from reading a story. A story has a beginning, middle, and end. A textbook has a Main Idea, followed by Details and Examples.

At the B1 level, you must learn to separate the "big picture" from the "small facts." When you read a paragraph, ask yourself: What is the one big thing the author wants me to learn here? Once you know the main idea, finding the small details becomes much easier.

Metacognitive Question: When you read a news article or a textbook in your native language, do you look at the title and the first paragraph to guess what it is about before reading the rest?

Tackling the 3 Core Question Types

At the B1 level, most of your points will come from mastering these three specific reading question types:

1. Factual Information

These ask "According to paragraph X, what is true about Y?" Strategy: Do not read the whole paragraph. Find the keyword "Y" in the paragraph, read the sentence that contains it, and find the option that matches.

2. Vocabulary in Context

These ask "The word X is closest in meaning to..." Strategy: Even if you know the word, look at the sentence. Replace the word with the answer choices. Which one makes the most logical sense in that specific sentence?

3. Pronoun Reference

These ask "The word 'they' refers to..." Strategy: Look at the sentence immediately before the pronoun. Find the plural or singular noun that logically performs the action described by the pronoun.

Read this introductory history text. Focus on identifying facts and understanding vocabulary from context.

Chocolate comes from the cacao tree, which grows in tropical environments near the equator. For thousands of years, people in Central America used cacao beans to make a bitter, spicy drink. They did not eat it as solid, sweet candy like we do today. In fact, the drink was considered very special and was often used in religious ceremonies.

Cacao beans were so valuable in ancient times that some cultures, like the Aztecs, used them as money. A person could buy food or clothes using the beans. When Spanish explorers arrived in the Americas in the 1500s, they discovered this bitter drink. They brought the cacao beans back to Europe, added sugar to the recipe, and transformed it into a sweet treat that quickly became popular around the world.

Explanation:

"They" refers to the Spanish explorers. If we look at the previous sentence ("When Spanish explorers arrived..."), "Spanish explorers" is the plural noun that makes logical sense to perform the action of bringing beans back to Europe.

Why Animals Migrate

Paragraph 1: Migration is the regular movement of animals from one location to another. Many different types of animals migrate, including birds, fish, and insects. Usually, these animals travel long distances to find a better environment. They migrate to survive when the seasons change and the weather becomes too harsh, meaning it is too cold or too dry to live comfortably.

Paragraph 2: Finding food is the primary reason for animal migration. In winter, the ground freezes in many northern areas, and food like insects and green plants disappears. Many bird species must fly south to warmer climates where food is still available. For instance, the Arctic Tern travels thousands of miles every year from the North Pole to the South Pole just to stay in areas with daylight and plenty of fish.

Paragraph 3: Another important reason for migration is to find a safe place to have babies. Some animals travel to special locations that protect their young from predators. Sea turtles, for example, swim to warm, shallow waters to give birth. How do these animals know where to go? Scientists believe that many migrating animals use the sun, the stars, and even the Earth's magnetic field to help them navigate across the globe.

Read the passage regarding the Water Cycle and answer the questions. (2 Attempts)

The Continuous Water Cycle

Water on Earth is constantly moving in a process called the water cycle. This process has no beginning and no end. First, energy from the sun heats up water in oceans, lakes, and rivers. This causes the liquid water to transform into an invisible gas called water vapor. This step is known as evaporation. The water vapor then rises high into the sky. As it goes higher, the air becomes colder, causing the vapor to change back into tiny liquid water drops, which form clouds. This is called condensation. Finally, when the clouds become too heavy, the water falls back to Earth as rain or snow, completing the cycle.

The History of the Olympic Games

Paragraph 1: The Olympic Games are the most famous sports competition in the world, but they started over two thousand years ago in ancient Greece. The first recorded games took place in 776 BCE in a town called Olympia. Unlike the modern games, which include many sports, the very first Olympic Games only had one event: a short running race. The games were held every four years and were very important to Greek culture because they were a religious festival to honor Zeus, the king of their gods.

Paragraph 2: For hundreds of years, the Olympics were very popular. However, in the year 393 CE, a Roman emperor named Theodosius stopped the games. He believed that the festival was wrong because it honored ancient Greek gods instead of the Roman religion. The games disappeared for over a thousand years. The stadiums in Olympia fell into ruin and were covered by dirt and grass.

Paragraph 3: The games were finally brought back to life in 1896 by a French man named Pierre de Coubertin. He believed that sports could bring different countries together in peace. He helped organize the first modern Olympic Games in Athens, Greece. Today, thousands of athletes from over 200 countries participate in the games. The modern Olympics are divided into the Summer Games and the Winter Games, giving athletes from all climates a chance to compete.
