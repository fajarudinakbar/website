Think about how a textbook is structured. A chapter usually starts with a broad introduction, followed by specific examples, causes, or categories. This is the Academic Blueprint.

To succeed at the B2 level, you must understand this structure. Instead of reading every single word slowly, learn to skim the first sentences of paragraphs to find the main idea, and scan the middle of paragraphs to find specific facts and details.

Tackling the 3 Core Question Types

At the B2 level, most of your points will come from mastering these three specific reading question types:

1. Factual Information

These ask "According to paragraph X, what is true about Y?" Strategy: Do not read the whole paragraph. Find the keyword "Y" in the paragraph, read the sentence that contains it, and find the option that matches.

2. Vocabulary in Context

These ask "The word X is closest in meaning to..." Strategy: Even if you know the word, look at the sentence. Replace the word with the answer choices. Which one makes the most logical sense in that specific sentence?

3. Pronoun Reference

These ask "The word 'they' refers to..." Strategy: Look at the sentence immediately before the pronoun. Find the plural or singular noun that logically performs the action described by the pronoun.

Read this introductory history text. Focus on identifying facts and understanding vocabulary from context.

Before the 15th century, books in Europe were copied entirely by hand. Monks and scribes spent months, sometimes years, meticulously writing and illustrating a single text. Because of this slow and labor intensive process, books were exceedingly rare and expensive. They were usually owned only by wealthy aristocrats, universities, and the church.

The invention of the mechanical movable type printing press by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 caused a revolution in information sharing. Gutenberg's machine used small metal blocks with individual letters on them. These blocks could be arranged to form words, coated with ink, and pressed onto paper. This innovation allowed books to be mass produced quickly and cheaply. As a result, literacy rates across Europe increased significantly, as ordinary people could finally afford to buy reading materials.

Explanation:

"They" refers to books. If we look at the previous sentence ("...books were exceedingly rare and expensive"), "books" is the plural noun that makes logical sense to be "owned" by wealthy people.

Read the passage on Animal Defense Mechanisms carefully and answer the 5 questions below. (2 Attempts)

Animal Camouflage and Mimicry

Paragraph 1: In the wild, survival often depends on an animal's ability to avoid being seen by predators. One of the most common and effective evolutionary strategies for this is camouflage. Camouflage occurs when an animal's physical appearance blends in with its natural environment. For example, the Arctic fox has white fur during the winter, which allows it to hide in the snow. When the seasons change and the snow melts, the fox sheds its white fur and grows a brown coat, enabling it to blend into the summer landscape of rocks and dirt.

Paragraph 2: A different, yet equally fascinating, survival strategy is mimicry. While camouflage is about hiding, mimicry is about deception. In mimicry, a harmless species evolves to look like a dangerous or poisonous species. Predators, having learned to avoid the dangerous animal, will also avoid the harmless mimic. A classic example of this is the Viceroy butterfly. It looks almost identical to the Monarch butterfly, which is toxic to birds. Because birds avoid the Monarch, they also leave the Viceroy alone, assuming it is poisonous too.

Paragraph 3: While both camouflage and mimicry serve the primary goal of protection, they function through different visual cues. Camouflage relies on background matching, making the animal virtually invisible. Mimicry, on the other hand, relies on bold, warning colors. Animals that use mimicry want to be seen clearly by predators so that their deceptive "warning" is not missed.

Urbanization in the 19th Century

The Industrial Revolution, which began in Great Britain in the late 18th century, profoundly changed society. Before this period, the majority of the population lived in rural farming villages. However, as new factories were built, they required a massive labor force. Millions of people migrated from the countryside to cities seeking employment in these new industries. This rapid migration is known as urbanization. Consequently, cities expanded incredibly fast. London's population, for instance, grew from roughly one million in 1800 to over six million by 1900. While this created immense wealth for factory owners, the rapid influx of people led to severe overcrowding, poor sanitation, and difficult living conditions for the working class.

Types of Human Memory

Paragraph 1: Psychologists generally divide human memory into three main stages: sensory memory, short term memory, and long term memory. Information from our environment first enters our sensory memory. This type of memory lasts for only a fraction of a second. It allows us to process sights, sounds, and smells just long enough to determine if they are important. If the brain decides the information is significant, it is transferred to the next stage.

Paragraph 2: Short term memory, often called working memory, holds information that we are actively thinking about. For example, if someone tells you a phone number, you use short term memory to remember it while you dial. However, short term memory has a very limited capacity. Research indicates that the average person can only hold about seven items (like numbers or words) in their short term memory at once. Furthermore, information here only lasts for about 20 to 30 seconds unless it is rehearsed or repeated.

Paragraph 3: For information to be retained permanently, it must be consolidated into long term memory. Unlike short term memory, long term memory has a seemingly limitless capacity and can store information for a lifetime. To move data from short term to long term storage, individuals often use techniques like deep processing, which involves associating new information with things they already know. Once information is stored in long term memory, it can be retrieved and brought back into working memory when needed.
