Write an Email

Topic: Tone, Register, and Social Conventions

Instructions: In the updated TOEFL iBT® Writing section, you will have exactly 7 minutes to read a prompt and write an email. This task measures your ability to communicate effectively in everyday academic and social situations.

Task 1 Cultural Connection: "Basa-basi" vs. Directness

In Indonesia, we often start emails to lecturers with long apologies: "Mohon maaf mengganggu waktunya..." (Sorry to disturb your time).

Question: Why might translating this directly into an English academic email lower your score on the "Social Conventions" rubric?

Strategy: English academic and professional emails value politeness but also directness. A literal translation like "Sorry to disturb your time" sounds unnatural and overly apologetic. A better approach is: "I hope this email finds you well," followed immediately by the purpose of the email.

Task 3 The "Register Trap"

You must write an email to the university housing office complaining about a broken heater. You start with: "Hey guys, my heater is totally busted..."
What is the issue with this opening?

The Rule: This is the Register Trap. "Hey guys" and "totally busted" are too informal (colloquial) for an official university office. You must match your tone (register) to the recipient. A better choice: "Dear Housing Office, I am writing to report that the heater in my room is broken."

Mastering the "Write an Email" Task

You have 7 minutes to read a scenario and write an email. The prompt will usually provide a scenario (e.g., academic, campus life, or professional) and list exactly three bullet points that you must include in your response.

The 4 Pillars of a Score of 5 (Highest Score):

  1. Elaboration: You must effectively address all parts of the prompt with sufficient detail. Do not just list the bullet points; explain them.
  2. Syntactic Variety & Vocabulary: Use a mix of simple, compound, and complex sentences. Use precise, idiomatic English words.
  3. Social Conventions: Use the correct register (formal vs. informal). Include a polite greeting (e.g., "Dear Professor Smith,"), a clear body, and an appropriate closing (e.g., "Best regards,"). Formulate requests and refusals politely.
  4. Accuracy: Make sure you have almost no grammatical or lexical errors. Minor typos expected under timed conditions are acceptable, but major sentence structure errors will lower your score.

Drafting Strategy (The 1-4-2 Rule for 7 Minutes):
• 1 Minute: Read the prompt carefully. Identify the audience (who are you writing to?) and the 3 required points.
• 4 Minutes: Write the email. Group the 3 bullet points logically into 1 or 2 body paragraphs.
• 2 Minutes: Proofread! Check for subject-verb agreement, spelling, punctuation, and tone.

Task 4 Strategy Check

Answer: The recipient determines the register and social conventions. Writing to a friend allows for casual language ("Hi," "Thanks!"), while writing to a professor or an editor requires formal language ("Dear Professor," "I would appreciate it if...").

Task 7 Paraphrase Challenge

Rewrite this informal complaint into a polite, formal request suitable for a university IT department:

"Fix the internet in the library ASAP. It's too slow and I can't do my homework."

Sample Rewrite:
"I am writing to report that the internet connection in the library is currently very slow, which is making it difficult to complete my assignments. Could you please look into this issue?"

Task 8 Analyzing "Basa-Basi"

In Indonesian emails, we often write "Demikian email ini saya sampaikan. Atas perhatiannya saya ucapkan terima kasih." How can you synthesize this sentiment into a natural English email closing without translating word-for-word?

Analysis: A natural English equivalent is simply:
"Thank you for your time and assistance." or
"I look forward to hearing from you soon."
Do not write: "Thus I deliver this email. For your attention I say thank you."

Task 10 Simulated Test: Write an Email

Prompt:

A new poetry magazine has asked its readers for submissions, and you decided to submit two of your poems. However, you had a problem using the online submission form, and you are not certain that your submissions were received.

Write an email to the editor of the magazine. In your email, do the following:

  • Tell the editor what you like about the new magazine.
  • Describe the problem you experienced.
  • Ask about the status of your submissions.

Write as much as you can and in complete sentences. (Recommended time: 7 minutes)

Sample High-Level Response (Score: 5)

Hi Editor, I hope you are well! I wanted to say that I really love the new Sunshine Poetry Magazine. It's refreshing to see such a diverse range of voices, and the layout is very engageing. However, I had some trouble with online submission form when I tried to submit two of my poems. After I uploaded the files and hit submit, the page just kept loading and loading, and eventually, I wasn't sure if my poems can sucessfully sent. Could you please confirm if you recieved my submissions? I'm really excited to be a part of your magazine and would appreciate any help you can provide to fix this issue. Looking forward to your response. Best regards, [Your Name]

*Note: This official ETS sample contains minor typos (e.g., "engageing", "recieved", "can sucessfully sent") which are expected under timed conditions and do not prevent a score of 5.
