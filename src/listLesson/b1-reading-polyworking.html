Polyworking: When One Job Is Not Enough

In recent years, more and more workers in Indonesia and around the world have started to take on more than one job. This new trend is called polyworking. It means having two or more main jobs at the same time, not just a small side hustle. For many people, polyworking is no longer a choice, but a way to survive.

Everyday Examples

Rahmad, a private employee in Tangerang, finishes his office job at 5 p.m. But his day does not end there. At night, he sells products online and creates promotional content on social media. Budiman, a teacher, spends his mornings in the classroom. In the afternoon, he works as an online motorcycle taxi driver to earn extra money. These stories show how common polyworking has become.

A survey in 2025 found that 18% of workers worldwide now have more than one job. In Indonesia, this trend is growing quickly. Rising prices and slow wage increases push many workers to look for extra income. The average monthly salary in Indonesia is about Rp3.09 million, but living costs are often Rp1–1.5 million higher. For many families, one job is simply not enough.

Why Do People Polywork?

At first, polyworking was seen as a smart strategy. It could help workers earn more, build new skills, and expand their professional networks. In creative industries, education, and technology, polyworking was even promoted as a way to strengthen careers. A study in 2024 showed that workers were motivated by financial needs, personal growth, and social goals.

But over time, the meaning has changed. For many Indonesians, polyworking is now a way to survive rising costs. More than half of workers live “paycheck to paycheck,” meaning their salary is spent almost immediately on daily needs. Without extra jobs, many cannot cover basic expenses.

Risks and Challenges

Polyworking may look like a sign of hard work, but it also hides serious problems. Long working hours can damage physical and mental health. Workers have less time to rest, study, or improve their skills. Instead of helping them move up socially, polyworking can trap them in exhaustion and burnout.

Another challenge is protection. Workers with multiple jobs often fall into a “grey area.” They are not fully protected like permanent employees, but they are also not recognized as independent entrepreneurs. This leaves them vulnerable if they face illness, accidents, or retirement without strong social security.

Economic Background

Indonesia’s economy grew by 5.04% in the third quarter of 2025, but this was still below the government’s target. At the same time, job opportunities did not grow as fast as the economy. Many industries, such as metal processing and data centers, are capital-intensive. They need machines and technology more than human labor. As a result, fewer jobs are created, even when the economy grows.

International organizations also warn of slower global growth. The World Bank, IMF, and OECD all predict weak investment and trade in the coming years. For developing countries like Indonesia, this means more pressure on workers.

What Can Be Done?

Experts say the government must act. First, minimum wages should reflect real living costs, not just political compromise. Second, social protection must cover flexible and multi-job workers, including health insurance and pensions. Third, training programs should help workers gain new skills for industries that are hiring.

Polyworking is not simply good or bad. The real question is: why do so many workers feel they must do it? If polyworking becomes necessary just to survive, it is a warning sign for the labor system. Workers should not be forced to take two or three jobs only to live a decent life.
