Speaker: What was the last online review you used? Andrew, what was the last online review you used?

Andrew: So, I spent two weeks in Canada, a glorious time, but just was very heavily reliant on, you know, we stayed in Airbnbs, we're constantly eating out, we're like finding places to whitewater raft and kayak, all the rest of it. So, using them a lot and sort of aware as I went that like, am I really in a position to trust them? How am I filtering them when I leave my own reviews on Airbnb? Am I being honest or am I somehow responding to norms?

Speaker: So, so what's the answer? Can you trust an online review?

Andrew: Well, it's better than the alternative. An absence of information is worse than what we have now. They are more useful than nothing, but they're also flawed in many ways. The most obvious problem is that it is subjective opinion.

Narrator: Researchers have looked at how reliable online reviews are. There is a gap between customer reviews and more objective measures of quality. Reviews often come from people who had very good or very bad experiences. This creates a J-shaped rating pattern.

Narrator: The best thing to do is to look for people who review a lot. These reviewers are less extreme and more balanced. Platforms also have different norms. Sharing economy platforms like Airbnb often have more generous reviews because users review each other, and there is a fear of retaliation.

3. Critical Discussion

  • Why does Andrew question whether he can trust online reviews?
  • What does Andrew mean when he says reviews are “better than nothing”?
  • Why are online reviews described as subjective?
  • What problem do researchers find with most online ratings?
  • Why are frequent reviewers considered more reliable?
Sample Answers:
  • He wonders if he is filtering them correctly and if he is being honest in his own reviews.
  • An absence of information is worse than having flawed information.
  • They are based on personal opinions rather than objective facts.
  • They have a "J-shaped" pattern because people only review very good or very bad experiences.
  • They are less extreme and provide more balanced opinions.

4.2 Speaking Focus – Evaluating Ideas

5.1 Choosing a Hotel

Scenario: You are booking a hotel online and see many five-star and one-star reviews, but very few in the middle.

  • Why might these reviews be extreme?
  • What should you check before deciding?
  • Would you trust the average rating? Why or why not?
Sample Ideas:
  • Reviews are extreme because people only write when they are very happy or very angry.
  • Check photos posted by users or read the detailed comments, not just the stars.
  • The average might be misleading if everyone is voting 1 or 5.

5.2 Being a Reviewer

Scenario: You had an "okay" (average) experience at a restaurant.

  • Would you leave a review? Why or why not?
  • How could your review help others?
  • What rating would you give?
Sample Ideas:
  • Most people don't review average experiences, but I should to help balance the ratings.
  • It helps show what a "normal" day looks like there.
  • 3 or 4 stars, explaining the good and bad points honestly.

5.3 Platform Differences

Scenario: You compare reviews on Airbnb vs. TripAdvisor. The Airbnb reviews are much kinder.

  • Why might Airbnb reviews be more positive?
  • How does reciprocity influence ratings?
  • Which platform would you trust more?
Sample Ideas:
  • Airbnb users review each other, so there is pressure to be nice.
  • Reciprocity means "I'm nice to you so you're nice to me."
  • TripAdvisor might be more honest because the hotel can't rate the guest back.
