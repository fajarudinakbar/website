Step 10: Integrated Summary Skill

Task 9: Short Summary

In 2–3 sentences, summarize why the color blue was important and difficult to use in art history.

The color blue was historically important because it symbolized wealth and status, but it was difficult to use because blue pigments were rare and expensive to produce. Artists often relied on precious stones or used very little blue until synthetic alternatives were developed in the 19th century.

Lecture Transcript

NARRATOR: Listen to a museum curator talk about the importance of the color blue.

CURATOR (MALE): So, as you know, our new exhibit is all about blue, I mean the color blue. We have a vast collection of paintings that show the importance of the color throughout history. It shows how the color blue became common among painters.

Nowadays, blue is a common color, appreciated by men and women alike, and used by many artists. However, blue colors aren’t easily made… You cannot simply take the blue of the sky or the sea, grind it up, and then throw it on a canvas – historically speaking, blue has been a difficult color to recreate.

Actually, blue pigments were rare. Now, what I mean by pigment is any natural coloring material from… an animal, plant, or something like a stone. Anyway, blue pigments are found in small amounts, so they carry a history of global trade and scientific invention.

Now, some of you may know that the color blue first appeared in Egyptian paintings and sculptures… What is impressive about this fact is that the pigments used by Egyptians were artificially made… The Egyptians combined different materials to make blue-like colors. The recipe for blue was then passed on to the Greeks and Romans, but it was eventually lost during the Middle Ages.

So, what did painters do to get blue pigments after the recipe was lost? Well, the only way artists could get blue pigments was by grinding up precious stones. Lapis lazuli is a deep blue stone from Afghanistan, and it made the ultramarine blue color; and another precious stone from France, known as azurite, made other shades of blue. One of the most popular shades that came from azurite was mountain blue.

Like I said before, blue pigments were rare and very expensive… They were also very complicated to make. Basically, artists had to spend many hours turning stones into color – that couldn’t have been easy! First, the stone was turned into powder. Then, melted wax, oils, and resin – or in other words, a sticky substance that comes from trees, were all added to the powder. Finally, artists had to wait patiently for the final color to show… it was a very long process for just a small amount of usable color.

So… artists would try to get around this process. Some used very little blue in their paintings; others used smalt. Smalt was a cheaper alternative to natural pigments, and it came from glass. And, well, many artists probably didn’t use blue pigments at all in their work! After all, the color blue was a symbol of luxury and wealth – sometimes even more so than gold. Rich people used to display paintings with a lot of blue in them to show off their wealth. This practice was especially popular during the Renaissance period… The Renaissance refers to a period in European history that lasted from the 14th to the 17th Century.

Fun fact, art historians believe that the famous Italian Renaissance painter Michelangelo left one of his paintings unfinished because he could not afford to buy more lapis lazuli to make blue.

Now, let’s look at this painting from the 18th Century… The Blue Boy from the English painter Gainsborough. You see a lot of blue in this painting, right? Some say that Gainsborough painted this portrait to win a bet – you know, someone challenged him to do something – the challenge was to use blue as the main color of a painting. As I said, it was uncommon to use a lot of blue pigments for paintings. However, as you can very well see, Gainsborough proved that person wrong.

Luckily, modern painters did not have to go through such lengths to find blue pigments. In 1824, the French government sponsored some research… the government paid scientists and artists to find cheaper alternatives to traditional methods of making blue. A new process was finally found and a synthetic substitute for lapis lazuli was created. This alternative had a great impact on the world of art. As you will see in the exhibition, 19th Century artists made more and more use of ultramarine colors.
